Having a will and a power of attorney (POA) in place is crucial for all adults in Ontario. These legal documents help ensure that your personal and financial affairs are handled according to your wishes, should you become incapacitated or pass away. Understanding the implications of not having these documents, as well as knowing when to keep them updated, can provide peace of mind and protect your loved ones.

## What Happens Without a Will: Intestacy

In Ontario, if you pass away without a will, your estate is distributed according to the laws of intestacy, set out in the Succession Law Reform Act. This means that instead of your assets being distributed according to your wishes, they will be divided according to a set formula. Typically, your spouse and children would be the first to inherit, but if you have neither, your estate could go to distant relatives or even escheat to the Crown if no relatives are found.

This lack of control can lead to outcomes that you may not have intended. For instance, you might have wanted to leave a specific item or sum to a friend or charity, but without a will, this wouldn’t happen automatically. [Wills and estates](/practice/wills-estates) laws also do not accommodate family dynamics, such as blended families or stepchildren, unless explicitly named in a will.

## Understanding Powers of Attorney

A power of attorney is a legal document that gives someone else the authority to make decisions on your behalf if you become unable to do so yourself. There are two main types: a power of attorney for property and a power of attorney for personal care.

1. **Power of Attorney for Property**: This type of POA allows someone to manage your financial affairs, such as banking, real estate transactions, and bill payments. It's important, especially if you have a complex estate or are frequently away from home. Our [real estate lawyers](/practice/real-estate) can assist in understanding how property-related decisions are managed with a POA.

2. **Power of Attorney for Personal Care**: This allows someone to make healthcare and personal decisions for you, such as medical treatments or living arrangements. It’s vital to choose someone you trust to ensure your personal wishes are respected.

## When to Update Your Documents

Once you have created a will and power of attorney, it's essential to keep them updated to reflect any major life changes. You might need to revise these documents when:

- You marry, divorce, or enter a common-law relationship, which can significantly affect your estate distribution.
- You have a child or a change in family dynamics, such as adoption.
- Your financial situation changes, such as acquiring or selling significant assets.
- Your relationships change, for example, if you wish to change your executor or attorney for property or personal care.

Failing to update your documents could lead to legal complications and may not accurately represent your current wishes.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

One common pitfall is preparing a will or power of attorney without consulting a lawyer. This can result in unclear or unenforceable documents. Another issue is failing to store these documents securely and inform your executors where they can be accessed.

Additionally, choosing the wrong person as your attorney or executor can cause issues if they do not act in your best interest. It’s crucial to select individuals based on trustworthiness and their ability to handle such responsibilities.
